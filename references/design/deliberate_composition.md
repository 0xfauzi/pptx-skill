# deliberate_composition v1

The anti-template skill. General craft only: concrete values - fonts, colors,
sizes, motifs - belong to the StyleGuide, never here. Where this skill and
the reference conflict, the reference wins.

## Principles

Composition is derived, not chosen. Read the content's own structure first: how
many entities, how many measures per entity, what is compared, what is
concluded. A ranked list of many entities across few measures IS a table read
down a column. A set of ranges around a comparison point IS one shared
horizontal axis. A sequence of arithmetic steps IS a left-to-right chain
terminating in a result. Never open by picking a layout and pouring content
into it.

One element dominates. Each slide has a single thing the eye must land on.
Everything else visibly reads as support: subordinate roles, muted ink,
marginal position. If you cannot name the dominant element in one phrase, the
composition is not done. When the content is genuinely several co-equal
blocks, do not tile them: rank the blocks by how directly each proves the
title, give the strongest the area and weight, and compress the rest into a
lighter supporting band - or order them into one directed argument
terminating in the result. Same-sized panels are a dashboard, not a slide.

Containers are exceptions. Separate regions with spacing steps and color.rule
hairlines. A box or fill must mark something genuinely exceptional; boxing
everything says nothing.

The composition occupies the whole canvas. Empty space at an edge must be a
decision you can name, not what was left when content ran out. Dense
at the top and abandoned at the bottom reads as unfinished, because it is.

Everything sits on an axis the composition already has. Few alignment edges,
held exactly; whitespace compressed where content is dense, generous where the
slide concludes. A callout or connector that does not visibly join what it
claims to join is decoration - the tell you are avoiding.

## Worked example: five co-equal exhibits

Five operational metrics, no natural hero, a title claiming margin durability.
Derived composition: the two margin series become one dominant chart under a
type.h2 heading, series in color.ink and color.accent1; the other three
compress into a supporting band below a color.rule hairline as figures with
type.caption labels in color.ink_muted at space.sm intervals.
Dominance was assigned by the title's claim, not by tiling.

## Worked example: arithmetic chain

Deal sources and uses: a left-to-right chain of steps joined by color.rule
connectors, each step a figure in type.body with a type.caption label, the
terminal result in type.emphasis over the slide's only color.accent1 mark,
sitting on the same baseline the chain established.

## Checklist

- Did the content's structure dictate the arrangement?
- Can you name the dominant element in one phrase?
- Does every box or fill mark a genuine exception?
- Is every callout and connector anchored to an existing edge?
- Is the whole canvas a decision, including its empty space?

## Anti-patterns: the named tells of a generated deck

- Card grid by default: content in equal rounded panels. Counter-example:
  regions separated by rules and whitespace, sized by importance.
- Icon + number + label KPI tiles. Counter-example: the figures in a table, or
  one dominant figure with its comparison beside it.
- Rounded-everything. Counter-example: square edges, any radius reserved for
  one accent the reference itself shows.
- Center-everything. Counter-example: flush-left text on shared edges; center
  only a single dominant figure.
- Rule-of-three monotony: three items because three looks tidy.
  Counter-example: the count the content actually has.
- Uniform spacing without tension. Counter-example: tight within groups, open
  between groups.
