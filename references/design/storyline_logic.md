# storyline_logic v1

General craft for sequencing a deck's argument. This skill shapes the plan -
the ordered slide goals and their assertion titles - never page geometry.
Register and phrasing observed in the reference are the StyleGuide's
property. Where this skill and the reference conflict, the reference wins.

## Principles

The pyramid: one governing thought, a key line, then support. The deck's
answer is stated first. The key line is the small set of arguments that
together prove it - mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive - and each
key-line argument becomes a section whose slides prove it in turn. Nothing
enters the plan that does not hold up something above it.

SCQA opens the deck. Situation: what the audience already accepts.
Complication: what changed to make the question urgent. Question, then
Answer - the governing thought the sections defend. An executive summary
goal may carry the whole SCQA up front.

Storyboard dot-dash before designing anything. Each dash is a slide title
written as an assertion; its dots are the evidence that slide must carry.
The dash decides what the page must prove, the dots what content it needs.
A dash without dots is an unsupported claim - cut it or find its evidence;
dots without a dash are homeless evidence.

Horizontal logic is the titles read in order. Adjacent titles connect
either deductively - each follows from the one before - or inductively, as
parallel members of one claim their section title states.

The flip test: read the plan's titles alone, in order. They must
reconstruct the argument as a coherent paragraph a reader could accept
without seeing a single exhibit. A plan that fails the flip test fails
before any page is designed.

Broken horizontal logic looks like: a label where an assertion should be; a
non sequitur between adjacent titles; evidence arriving before the claim it
supports; an answer to a question the setup never raised; a section whose
titles do not add up to its key-line argument.

## Worked example: SCQA opening sequence

Four goals open a diagnostic. Situation: "The service now handles record
volume on stable funding." Complication: "Response rates are falling while
complaints rise." The question - can both trends be reversed? - is left
implicit. Answer: "Coordinated action on intake, capacity, and outcomes
reverses both trends." The sections that follow take the answer's three
nouns in order, one key-line argument each; later goals' kickers name their
sections, keeping the sequence legible.

## Worked example: flip test repair

A draft plan reads "Findings", "Benchmarks", "Recommendations" - labels, no
argument. Rewritten, the titles alone carry it: "Productivity trails every
benchmarked peer", then "The gap is concentrated in follow-through", then
"Closing follow-through alone recovers most of the gap", then "A three-step
program closes it within the year". Each title inherits its subject from
the previous title's conclusion, and the four read as one paragraph.

## Checklist

- Does every goal hold up a key-line argument, and every key-line argument
  the governing thought?
- Does the opening carry situation, complication, and answer?
- Does each dash have dots, and each dot a dash?
- Do the titles alone, in plan order, read as the argument?
- Does each section's title sequence add up to its section claim?

## Anti-patterns

- Label titles in the plan: the flip test fails before design begins.
- Evidence goals scheduled before the claim they support.
- Two goals asserting the same thing in different words.
- A resolution answering a question the setup never raised.
- Sections carved by data source or workstream instead of by argument.
